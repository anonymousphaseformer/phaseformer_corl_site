Phaseformer

Multimodal Policies without Iterative Compute or Stochastic Inference

(A) Trained with a velocity-based MSE loss alone, a deterministic policy averages modes. (B) Phaseformer injects task progress ρ into every block through AdaLN and adds a regularizer built from the score \(\nabla\log p(\mathbf{x})\) of the robot state distribution at each phase.

Phaseformer is a single-step, deterministic behavior cloning policy that handles multimodal demonstrations with no diffusion and no noise sampling, and sits on the latency–performance Pareto front for CPU control.

Abstract

Deterministic behavior cloning policies struggle to capture multimodal robotic action distributions, suffering from mode averaging. Accepted solutions transform stochastic samples into robot actions, often via iterative computation (e.g. diffusion and flow matching). Flow matching backbones themselves are deterministic however, leading us to ask: why do flow matching models succeed at capturing flows between endpoint distributions while deterministic policies fail in similar situations? In this work, we prove that flow matching is a special case of behavior cloning, show that conditioning a deterministic policy on task progress lets it reproduce multimodal demonstrations whose start states vary, and characterize a score-based regularizer that pulls the policy toward the locally dominant demonstrated velocity where modes overlap. We demonstrate this empirically on multimodal robot navigation and in-plane manipulation tasks. Our findings indicate that lightweight, single-step deterministic policies can solve multimodal tasks while providing a Pareto-optimal trade-off for ultra-low latency, CPU based control.

How it works

1

Phase injection

The policy is conditioned on task progress ρ ∈ [0, 1], the role time plays in flow matching. Phaseformer injects ρ into every transformer block through AdaLN, effectively giving each stage of the task its own policy. At run time ρ comes from a clock or a small learned progress estimator. It is never sampled.

2

Score-based regularizer

Where modes overlap, the L2 (MSE) loss alone averages them. We keep that loss and add an L1 regularizer on the velocity error projected onto the score \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log\hat p_\rho\) of the demonstrations at that phase. It pulls predictions toward the locally dominant (median) velocity. The score is precomputed in closed form and is not used at inference.

3

Stochasticity from the starts

A deterministic policy can only send two starts to different modes if the starts differ. Spread in the demonstrated start states provides that; variation along the path does not. The randomness lives in the training data, not at inference.

$$\phi^\ast=\arg\min_\phi\;\mathbb{E}\Big[\underbrace{\big\|\pi_\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{O},\rho)-\mathbf{u}\big\|^2}_{\text{L2 behavior cloning loss}}\;+\;\lambda\,\underbrace{\big|\big(\mathbf{u}-\pi_\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{O},\rho)\big)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log\hat p_\rho(\mathbf{x})\big|}_{\text{L1 score regularizer}}\Big]$$

\(\mathbf{u}\): demonstrated action (a state velocity), \(\mathbf{x}\): robot state, \(\mathcal{O}\): observation, \(\rho\): phase, \(\hat p_\rho\): kernel density estimate of the demonstrated states at phase \(\rho\).

Demonstrations already look like a flow

Viewed over phase, a set of demonstrations looks just like a flow matching probability path: a start distribution that evolves and splits into modes. Flow matching gives its network the phase; standard behavior cloning does not, so it averages velocities across phases and modes. We prove that flow matching is phase-conditioned behavior cloning on straight-line demonstrations.

Push-T: on the latency–performance Pareto front

We train four backbones on Diffusion Policy's human Push-T demonstrations, each with every combination of phase and score: two MLPs (0.8M and 1.8M parameters) and two transformers (1.8M and 3.4M). On a CPU, every phase+score model lies on the Pareto front, which runs from 0.15 ms up to ACT's 5 ms per control step. MLP-med wide+phase+score scores 0.67 in 0.39 ms, inside a 1 kHz control loop; the only baseline that fits that budget is open-loop MIP, at 0.41. TF-large+phase+score reaches 0.72 in 2.1 ms. Diffusion Policy scores highest (0.96 open loop), but needs 432 ms per control step on the CPU and 17 ms on the GPU, even with its 8-step chunk amortizing each decision.
